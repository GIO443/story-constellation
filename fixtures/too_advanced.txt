Oliver the Cartographer

Oliver considered himself the preeminent cartographer of his own backyard. Each afternoon he charted its topography with scrupulous exactitude: the perimeter hedge, the desiccated birdbath, the labyrinthine roots of the venerable sycamore.

One blustery afternoon, a capricious gust wrenched the map from his grasp and deposited it beyond the garden fence, in the brambly gully he had never possessed the audacity to chart. Oliver was disconsolate, for the map represented an entire summer of assiduous observation.

He endeavored to retrieve it with the rake, but the implement proved insufficient; the map remained tantalizingly inaccessible, ensnared in the vegetation. He attempted to insinuate himself between the fence palings, but succeeded only in acquiring a scratch and a formidable sense of futility.

Then Oliver recollected the fundamental axiom of cartography: before one traverses unfamiliar terrain, one must first survey it. He ascended the compost bin and scrutinized the gully methodically, as though it were merely another region awaiting documentation. There — a gap beneath the elderberry, an unimpeded route to the map.

He navigated the passage with deliberation, retrieved his map, and returned to the garden triumphant. That evening, at the kitchen table, he appended a new region to his chart, annotated in his most punctilious handwriting: The Gully. Previously unexplored. Not so inscrutable after all.
